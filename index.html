No. 01Art Direction

Paula Scher paints the same flood, again

Paula Scher (Pentagram) with James McMullan for Lake

Fifty years after art-directing the German-British rock band’s debut, Scher was called back by the reunited lineup to design their new album, ‘Four’ — commissioning 89-year-old illustrator James McMullan to paint another surreal flood, this time starring a frog instead of a cat. It’s a rare, well-documented case study in how a strong conceptual system (illustrated chaos, one motif, one collaborator) can outlive a band, a label, and four decades without going stale.

No. 02Campaign

Prada and Gentle Monster fracture the triangle

Prada × Gentle Monster

The two houses’ first eyewear collaboration reworks Prada’s signature triangle logo into angular, sculptural frames across three styles, launching first in Japan before rolling out across Asian markets. Worth studying as a masterclass in co-branding restraint — two very different design languages, Milanese minimalism and Seoul maximalism, sharing one object without either one flattening the other.

No. 03Brand Identity

Ronnie Scott’s identity learns to improvise

Dan Cottrell Studio for Ronnie Scott’s

Rather than lock the 66-year-old Soho jazz club into a fixed mark, Dan Cottrell Studio built a visual system that behaves more like a performance than a template — type and layout shift set to set, borrowing the rhythm and spontaneity of the music inside. A sharp reference for any heritage culture client whose brand keeps getting flattened into a rigid guideline deck.

No. 04Packaging

Cowshed goes wild with botanical monotypes

Otherway for Cowshed

Facing a crowded wellness shelf now packed with Aesop, Byredo and a wave of ingredient-obsessed newcomers, Otherway reconnects the Soho House spa brand with its rural Somerset roots via loose, unrepeatable botanical monotypes by Helsinki artist Reeta Ek. The restraint is the point — understated type and generous space give the illustration room to feel wilder and more expressive than the category usually allows.

No. 05Packaging

Bare Earth is branded like a forest floor

Blanca Doba

A speculative identity for a fictional natural body-care line, built not from mood-board references but from the actual textures of resin, clay, moss and bark — each material dictating a piece of the visual logic rather than just decorating it. A good exercise in what “building a world” really means when the brief says “sustainable” and the deck is tempted to reach for a leaf icon instead.

No. 06Brand Identity

A 750-year-old market designs its own toolkit

Bob Design with Croydon School of Art for Surrey Market

Rather than parachute in a fixed logo, Bob Design ran workshops and public consultations with Croydon art students to excavate the market’s own history, drawing a new typeface straight from archival market signage and turning found wayfinding arrows into a deliberately wiggly, hand-made system. A useful precedent for any civic or heritage brief where the community’s own visual language is the strategy, not just the research phase.

No. 07Brand Identity

Sporting CP’s lion sheds its shield

Jones Knowles Ritchie for Sporting Clube de Portugal

For the Lisbon club’s 120th anniversary, JKR strips away the outer shield to put the lion front and center, recolors it from yellow to white, and pairs the simplified crest with a custom typeface, Sporting Sans, built with F37. A clean study in how much equity a century-old sports mark can shed — and how much sharper the remaining work can look — once you stop trying to preserve everything at once.

No. 08Brand Identity

Rugiet swaps wellness pastels for assertive orange

Matt Willey (Pentagram) for Rugiet

Pentagram’s identity for the men’s-health platform breaks from the category’s usual soft, pastel wellness palette in favor of a vivid, intense “Rugiet Orange” and a bespoke vertical typeface, Rugiet Tall, built for short, punchy, upright statements like “Rise Up.” It’s a useful reminder that “clinical credibility” doesn’t have to mean quiet — sometimes the more assertive, less apologetic option is the one that actually differentiates.

No. 09Typography

Koto starts its own type foundry

Koto (CCType)

The global studio behind identities for Stack Overflow, Amazon and Deezer launches CCType, a foundry built specifically to meet brand-driven type demands, debuting with CC Timeline — a display serif blending early British metal type with modernist Swiss and German photo-typesetting. Worth watching as more studios follow Koto’s lead and turn their in-house type work into a standalone product line rather than a one-off brand asset.

Four things to chew on this week
Thomas Wakeford (Freitag) It’s Nice That · Interview · ~14 min read

How Freitag keeps its cool

Freitag’s creative director walks through three decades of campaigns built on Dada-esque humor and real employees instead of models — from zipping the founders into sleeping bags for a fake business trip to Tokyo, to casting every “Laura” the brand could find for a bag named Laura. A genuinely useful masterclass in how a strong founding story can license stranger, funnier creative work than most brands ever let themselves make.

Read on It’s Nice That →
Craig Mod Design Matters · Podcast · ~55 min listen

Craig Mod on memory, loss and the paths we travel

Writer, photographer and publisher Craig Mod talks with Debbie Millman about his memoir Things Become Other Things, and the long, on-foot process of making work that’s inseparable from walking, noticing and getting lost. A good listen for anyone whose process has gotten too screen-bound — a reminder that some of the best creative thinking happens away from the desk entirely.

Listen on Design Matters →
Samar Maakaroun (interview) Eye Magazine · Interview · ~18 min read

Reputations: Samar Maakaroun

The latest in Eye’s long-running Reputations series profiles Pentagram partner Samar Maakaroun on her path between London and Beirut, her early career as a stage designer, and what it means to design at the junction of two cultures instead of picking one. A sharp read on cultural fluency as a design skill in its own right, not just a biographical detail.

Read on Eye Magazine →
Marianne Olaleye (JAIKU) Creative Boom · Insight · ~9 min read

Why we shouldn’t obsess with Gen Z

JAIKU founder Marianne Olaleye argues that brands’ reflexive chase for youth ignores a huge, underrepresented, high-spending older audience — and previews a storytelling summit built around cross-generational and cross-industry perspective instead. A useful nudge for any strategy deck that defaults to youth culture as the aesthetic ideal without asking who’s actually being left out of the room.
